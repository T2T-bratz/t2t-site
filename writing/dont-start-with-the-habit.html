Personal growth and habits

Don't start with the habit

Resolution season fails before it starts, and it's not a willpower problem. It's a sequencing problem. Map the week you actually have before you add anything to it.

02 December 2026 · 3 min read · Drafted by AI, reviewed by Brad

In about four weeks a lot of people are going to try to bolt fifteen habits on at once. Most of those attempts will be over by February. That's not a willpower problem. It's a sequencing problem.

Don't start with the habit

When someone asks me how to build a habit, I don't talk about the habit. I ask questions, and they're all about awareness.

What does your current week actually look like? Not the one you'd describe at a dinner party. The real one. Where does your energy peak and where does it crater? What's already happening on autopilot, good and bad? What are you doing at 9pm that you'd never schedule at 9am?

Most people can't answer those with any precision, and that's the point. You're trying to add a new behaviour to a system you've never looked at. Of course it doesn't stick.

Current state, future state

The first step is a map. Two of them, actually.

The first is your current state. For one normal week, write down what you did in each block of the day. No judging, no fixing, just recording. It's tedious and it's the most useful hour you'll spend this quarter.

The second is what I call an ideal calendar. Not an aspirational one with 5am workouts and three hours of deep work. An honest one. Where would the things you say matter actually fit, given the energy you really have at each point in the day?

Lay them side by side. The gap between the two maps is your habit list. It's usually short. That's good news.

Respect the load

Here's where the research earns its keep. Cognitive load is real and it's finite. John Sweller's work on cognitive load theory came out of education research in the late 1980s, but the core finding travels: when you ask a mind to hold too many new things at once, performance on all of them drops. Fifteen new habits in January is exactly that.

James Clear makes a related point in Atomic Habits: the habits that survive are the ones small enough to do on your worst day, attached to something you already do. BJ Fogg's Tiny Habits research at Stanford says the same thing from a different angle. Start embarrassingly small, anchor it to an existing routine, and let it grow.

I'd add one thing from my own experience, which is recovery. Building a habit costs energy, and that energy has to come from somewhere. If you don't deliberately protect sleep and downtime while you're adding something new, the new thing is what gets dropped first.

Start with the efficiency habit

If you can only pick one habit from your gap list, pick the one that gives time back. Not the most impressive one. The one that frees up a block of your week.

For me, years ago, it was time blocking, and keeping to it. Not because time blocking is magic (Cal Newport has written more usefully on it than I will) but because once it held, it dominoed. Fewer re-made decisions meant more energy at 4pm. More energy at 4pm meant the gym happened. The gym happening meant better sleep, and so on.

Efficiency habits domino. Aspirational habits mostly don't. Start where the dominoes are.

In January I'll walk through the second map and how to pick the first habit off the gap list. By then you'll have real data instead of a resolution.

Something to try this week

Before the end of this week, record one normal week as it happens, block by block, in whatever tool you'll actually open. Don't change anything yet. Just look.
